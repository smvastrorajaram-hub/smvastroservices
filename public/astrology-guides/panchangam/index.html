The Five Parts of Panchangam: Tithi, Vara, Nakshatra, Yoga and Karana

Understand the five Panchangam limbs and why their end times depend on the Sun, Moon, location and the chosen day convention.

What Panchangam records

Panchangam is a traditional Hindu almanac that combines five astronomical-calendar elements. The word is commonly explained as “five limbs”. A daily Panchangam entry is not simply a list of fixed values for a Gregorian date: the relevant elements change at different times, and the displayed day may follow a local sunrise convention.

For a meaningful comparison, note the location, time zone, sunrise rule and whether the page shows the value at sunrise or the transition time during the day. Two Panchangams can appear different if one shows the running element and the other shows the element at sunrise.

The five limbs

ElementWhat it describesHow it changes
TithiA lunar day based on the angular separation of the Sun and MoonEach tithi spans 12 degrees of their relative longitude; it can begin or end at any local clock time
VaraThe weekday in the local calendarIt follows the day boundary used by the almanac, commonly linked to sunrise in traditional Panchangam practice
NakshatraThe Nakshatra occupied by the MoonIt changes when the Moon crosses the next sidereal Nakshatra boundary
YogaA division derived from the sum of the Sun's and Moon's longitudesIt changes when that combined longitude crosses the next 13°20′ division
KaranaHalf of a tithiOne karana spans 6 degrees of the Sun–Moon separation; the sequence follows a traditional repeating pattern

Why end times matter

A tithi can end after sunrise or before the next sunrise. The same is true for a Nakshatra, Yoga or Karana. A Panchangam that prints “Tithi until 14:20” is describing a transition time, not saying the tithi lasts only for the first part of a date. If a date is used for a traditional observance, the prescribed rule may depend on whether the element is present at sunrise, during a specific period or at another rule-defined time.

Do not assume every festival or observance uses the same selection rule. Some traditions consider sunrise presence; others use a specified moment, conjunction or interval. A local calendar should explain the rule when the result is intended for a particular observance.

Location and time-zone settings

Sunrise and sunset depend on latitude, longitude and date. Time-zone rules determine how the astronomical instant is displayed on a civil clock. Therefore, the same global event may have a different local clock time in two cities. Selecting a nearby city is not always equivalent to selecting the actual birthplace or observance location, especially across a wide region.

When checking a calendar result, verify the city, coordinates, local date and displayed time zone. If the page provides the transition time in local time, compare it with a second source using the same location before assuming there is a calculation error.
